Industry Specialization and County Household Income

One question, two sectors, and a descriptive county comparison

Published

Do counties with above-average manufacturing or professional/business-services employment shares also have higher median household incomes? This is a useful check on a common development-policy assumption: attracting a particular industry and improving residents’ living standards are different outcomes.

I compare 2024 private-sector workplace employment from BLS QCEW with Census ACS 2020–2024 resident household income, expressed in 2024 dollars. The study covers the contiguous 48 states and Washington, DC. This is an unadjusted geographic association, not a test of whether an industry caused an income difference.

Locate specialization before comparing incomes

A county’s location quotient (LQ) divides its sector employment share by that sector’s published U.S. private-employment share:

\[ LQ_{ic}=\frac{E_{ic}/E_c}{E_{i,US}/E_{US}}. \]

An LQ of 2 means twice the U.S. share, not twice as many jobs or twice the productivity. Private ownership is used in both denominators; national benchmarks come directly from published U.S. totals. Figure 1 establishes where each sector is concentrated. It cannot, by itself, establish an income relationship.

Manufacturing specialization forms a broad belt through the Midwest and Southeast, while business-service concentration is more scattered. This is relative local reliance: a small county can be dark without having many jobs, and large polygons represent land rather than workers. These contrasting geographies motivate examining each sector separately rather than treating all specialization as one route to prosperity.

Test the income relationship directly

For each sector, Figure 2 compares counties below the national employment share (LQ < 1) with those at or above it (LQ ≥ 1). Each county receives equal weight. Unlike separate industry and income maps, this grouping directly displays their relationship.

The median of county household-income estimates is $3,942 lower in the above-average manufacturing group, but $17,522 higher in the above-average business-services group. The opposite directions answer the question: in these data, above-average sector employment shares coincide with different income patterns, depending on the sector. The overlapping distributions also show that an industry label does not identify every county’s income level.

Dropping income estimates whose 90% margin of error exceeds 30% of the estimate gives differences of $4,498 lower and $18,019 higher. Restricting both sectors to the same 2632 counties gives $4,104 lower and $17,856 higher. These checks assess sensitivity to precision and sample composition; they are not significance tests.

What the comparison can support

Professional/business services includes administrative support and waste services as well as professional and technical work. It is not a pure high-skill category. QCEW measures workplace jobs; household income describes residents, including commuters and people receiving nonwage income. Education, household composition, and living costs are not controlled for here. The observed differences therefore do not show that one sector raises incomes, attracts workers, or reduces unemployment.

Gray map areas remain visible as missing or suppressed, never zero. The income chart includes 2697 manufacturing observations and 2934 business-service observations; differing disclosure coverage can affect results. No single-year 2024 income change or population trend is inferred from these five-year estimates.

Takeaway

Above-average manufacturing shares coincide with lower median county household income in this comparison; above-average business-service shares coincide with higher income. That is an industry-specific descriptive pattern, not a causal ranking of development strategies. The maps identify local industrial concentrations; the grouped income distributions supply the direct evidence about residents. A county should investigate who holds the jobs and the local cost of living before treating specialization as a promise of prosperity.
