Where Are America’s Industries, and What Do They Mean for Local Economies?

Industrial Structure, Jobs, Wages, Income, and Regional Change Across U.S. Counties

regional-economics
GIS
labor-markets
Author

Zhenxin Li

Published

October 19, 2026

Across the United States, counties differ widely in the industries that support their local economies. How does a county’s industrial structure relate to its economy and population?

To answer that question, this analysis maps major industries across U.S. counties and examines how industrial specialization is associated with employment, wages, household income, population, and educational attainment—and how these patterns have changed over time.

Using county-level data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics’ Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages (QCEW) and the Census Bureau’s American Community Survey (ACS), this study combines GIS maps, location quotients, and descriptive comparisons to explore these relationships.

The findings reveal geographic associations, not causal effects.

The Geography of America’s Industries

Each industry leaves its own geographic footprint. The multi-panel map below shows each major sector’s share of private‑sector county employment. Some industries are widely dispersed across most counties, while others cluster tightly in specific regions.

The broad industrial landscape

These county-level maps show each major industry’s share of private-sector jobs in 2024, revealing sharply different geographic footprints. Trade and construction appear broadly nationwide, while education-health jobs anchor many small rural counties. Manufacturing concentrates in the Midwest and South. Information, finance and professional services cluster in large metro areas, and resource extraction only lights up resource-rich counties. Note that high employment share can reflect tiny total jobs in sparse rural counties.

Two case studies: manufacturing and professional services

Manufacturing builds physical goods and sustains sprawling supply chains, often tied to community debates about good local jobs. Professional and business services include consulting and technical work that powers modern firms. This analysis single out these sectors for comparison as they stand for two currently vital economic paths: goods production and knowledge work. Their contrasting geographic clusters create a natural lens to investigate whether industry specialization translates into better economic outcomes for county residents.

A location quotient (LQ) compares an industry’s share of local private employment with its share of national private employment:

\(LQ_{ic} =\frac{\text{Industry employment}_{ic}/\text{Private employment}_{c}}{\text{National industry employment}_{i}/\text{National private employment}}\)

An LQ above 1 means that the industry represents a larger share of local employment than it does nationally. It does not necessarily indicate a large absolute number of jobs or higher productivity.

Manufacturing specialization while professional‑business services cluster heavily in major metro areas. Counties with manufacturing specialization are scattered across the Midwest and rural South,concentrating across many non‑metropolitan counties. Professional-business services specialization, cluster heavily in major metro areas. High LQ signals local job concentration in that industry.

How Does Industrial Geography Relate to Household Income, Wages, and Jobs?

We often assume more local jobs equal richer residents and more job opportunities, is that really the case?

Does manufacturing specialization correspond to higher household income?

This scatter plot compares counties’ manufacturing location quotient to resident median household income. A modest manufacturing presence can support household incomes by supplying solid blue-collar work. But counties overly specialized in manufacturing face risks from sectoral shocks and limited alternative job opportunities, so income no longer rises and may even fall. The scattered points show manufacturing jobs alone cannot determine residents’ prosperity. Because workers commute across county boundaries, factory job concentration does not guarantee local households capture the economic gains from that industrial activity.

How do average weekly wages differ across sectors?

Are professional service jobs consistently better paid than manufacturing roles? This graph compares county average weekly wages across the two sectors. Professional and business services have a higher typical county wage, rewarding specialized technical and analytical skills. But wages vary widely within each sector from county to county. Remember these figures are county averages, hiding big gaps between individual workers. Industry sets the baseline for pay, yet local economic conditions also shape what workers ultimately earn.

Where are labor-market conditions stronger or weaker?

These two maps show clear regional disparities tied to local industry composition. Counties with strong professional and business services tend to display low unemployment and high employment-population ratios, reflecting abundant skilled work. Many rural counties reliant on manufacturing or resource industries often see elevated unemployment and weaker adult employment rates. Some tourism-dependent counties show volatile labor outcomes. This reveals that the industrial footprint we mapped earlier shapes residents’ job prospects as the mix of local jobs determines both unemployment levels and broader workforce participation.

Where Are People and Skills Concentrated?

We now examine how industries shape a region’s ability to attract residents. Population and human capital reveal the pull of local economies: population size reflects the scale of the labor market, while educational attainment captures formal schooling among residents. The share of adults aged 25 and older holding a bachelor’s degree or higher acts as our proxy for one dimension of human capital. This statistic cannot directly measure practical skills, work experience, labor productivity, or differences in education quality.

Population change

Counties with expanding professional and business services often see population gains, as knowledge jobs draw working-age households. Many rural manufacturing counties face population losses, because shrinking factory employment reduces the region’s pull. Red regions lose residents while blue regions grow. Please note that these percentage changes combine migration, births and deaths and they cannot be attributed solely to people moving for jobs.

Educational attainment and industrial specialization

This scatterplot visualizes the mutual attraction between educated workers and local industries. The rising blue trend line for professional and business services shows that counties with more college graduates become increasingly specialized in knowledge-intensive work: the industry draws educated talent, and this talent in turn supports further industry growth. Manufacturing’s red inverted-U curve tells a different story. Manufacturing specialization peaks at moderate bachelor-degree shares; in counties with very high educational attainment, manufacturing location quotients decline, as highly educated residents tend not to cluster in manufacturing-specialized regions.

How Are Regional Industries Changing?

Maps of industrial concentration show where different industries are specialized, but they capture only one point in time. Comparing county-level employment in 2019 and 2024 reveals where industries expanded or contracted, helping us understand how regional economic structures have shifted. Examining manufacturing alongside professional and business services also highlights whether different types of local economies followed similar or contrasting employment trends.

Manufacturing employment change

This county-level map reveals uneven shifts in manufacturing employment from 2019 to 2024. Parts of the traditional industrial heartland experienced manufacturing job losses, while scattered counties across the South and some western regions saw manufacturing employment expand. These divergent outcomes reshape regional industrial attractiveness: areas losing factory jobs become less appealing to production workers, while counties gaining manufacturing work draw residents seeking blue-collar roles.

Do manufacturing and professional services grow in the same places?

A minority of counties expand employment in both industries. More commonly, growth happens in only one sector: some counties gain manufacturing jobs without professional service growth, while others expand professional services with weak manufacturing performance. Some counties lose jobs in both sectors. These varied trajectories reveal the two industries favor different locations and draw different worker groups. (This metric only records net change and cannot isolate pandemic or policy effects.)

What Do These Patterns Mean for Communities?

Industrial structure shapes local job opportunities, but specialization does not guarantee prosperity. Manufacturing is prominent in many Midwestern and Southern counties, while professional and business services concentrate in metropolitan areas. However, household income also depends on wages, education, labor-market conditions, and population trends. Employment growth from 2019 to 2024 varies across counties and industries. Understanding local economies therefore requires looking beyond dominant industries to assess job access, pay, and long-term opportunities. These patterns show regional differences, not causal relationships.

Key Takeaways

  • Specialization is not prosperity: A high location quotient indicates relative industry concentration, not necessarily more jobs or higher household income.
  • Employment, wages, and income measure different things: Workplace-based job and wage data do not directly capture residents’ financial well-being.
  • People and industries vary across regions: Population trends, educational attainment, and labor-market conditions provide important context for local economies.
  • Regional growth is uneven: Manufacturing and professional and business services follow different employment trajectories across counties.

For residents and policymakers, understanding a local economy means asking not only which industries dominate, but also what jobs are available, how they are paid, who benefits, and how opportunities change over time.

For residents and policymakers, the practical question is not simply “Which industry is best?” It is: What kinds of jobs are available locally, who can access them, how are they paid, and how is the local mix of opportunities changing?

download_qcew <- function(year) {

  cache_file <- file.path(
    data_dir,
    paste0("qcew_", year, "_annual.csv")
  )

  if (file.exists(cache_file) &&
      file.info(cache_file)$size > 1000000) {
    return(
      readr::read_csv(
        cache_file,
        show_col_types = FALSE,
        progress = FALSE
      )
    )
  }

  zip_url <- sprintf(
    "https://data.bls.gov/cew/data/files/%s/csv/%s_annual_singlefile.zip",
    year, year
  )

  zip_file <- file.path(
    data_dir,
    paste0("qcew_", year, "_annual_singlefile.zip")
  )

  utils::download.file(
    zip_url,
    destfile = zip_file,
    mode = "wb",
    quiet = TRUE
  )

  zip_contents <- utils::unzip(zip_file, list = TRUE)

  csv_member <- zip_contents$Name[
    grepl("\\.csv$", zip_contents$Name, ignore.case = TRUE)
  ]

  if (length(csv_member) == 0) {
    stop("No CSV file was found in the QCEW archive for ", year)
  }

  extracted_files <- utils::unzip(
    zip_file,
    files = csv_member[1],
    exdir = data_dir
  )

  if (length(extracted_files) == 0 ||
      !file.exists(extracted_files[1])) {
    stop("Could not extract the QCEW CSV for ", year)
  }

  result <- readr::read_csv(
    extracted_files[1],
    show_col_types = FALSE,
    progress = FALSE
  )

  readr::write_csv(result, cache_file)

  result
}


standardize_qcew <- function(data, year) {

  names(data) <- tolower(names(data))

  required_columns <- c(
    "area_fips",
    "own_code",
    "industry_code",
    "annual_avg_emplvl",
    "annual_avg_wkly_wage"
  )

  missing_columns <- setdiff(required_columns, names(data))

  if (length(missing_columns) > 0) {
    stop(
      "The QCEW file for ", year,
      " is missing required columns: ",
      paste(missing_columns, collapse = ", "),
      ". Inspect the downloaded file before continuing."
    )
  }

  data |>
    mutate(
      area_fips = str_pad(
        as.character(area_fips),
        width = 5,
        side = "left",
        pad = "0"
      ),
      own_code = as.character(own_code),
      industry_code = str_trim(as.character(industry_code)),
      annual_avg_emplvl = readr::parse_number(
        as.character(annual_avg_emplvl)
      ),
      annual_avg_wkly_wage = readr::parse_number(
        as.character(annual_avg_wkly_wage)
      ),
      year = as.integer(year)
    )
}


get_county_acs <- function(year) {

  acs_variables <- c(
    total_population = "B01003_001",
    median_household_income = "B19013_001",
    population_16plus = "B23025_001",
    labor_force = "B23025_003",
    employed = "B23025_004",
    unemployed = "B23025_005",
    population_25plus = "B15003_001",
    bachelor = "B15003_022",
    master = "B15003_023",
    professional = "B15003_024",
    doctorate = "B15003_025"
  )

  acs <- tidycensus::get_acs(
    geography = "county",
    variables = acs_variables,
    year = year,
    survey = "acs5",
    geometry = FALSE,
    output = "wide",
    key = census_key
  )

  expected_columns <- paste0(names(acs_variables), "E")

  missing_columns <- setdiff(expected_columns, names(acs))

  if (length(missing_columns) > 0) {
    stop(
      "ACS output is missing expected estimate columns: ",
      paste(missing_columns, collapse = ", ")
    )
  }

  acs |>
    transmute(
      GEOID = as.character(GEOID),
      NAME,
      acs_year = as.integer(year),
      total_population = total_populationE,
      median_household_income = median_household_incomeE,
      population_16plus = population_16plusE,
      labor_force = labor_forceE,
      employed = employedE,
      unemployed = unemployedE,
      population_25plus = population_25plusE,
      bachelor_plus = bachelorE + masterE +
        professionalE + doctorateE,
      unemployment_rate = if_else(
        labor_forceE > 0,
        unemployedE / labor_forceE,
        NA_real_
      ),
      employment_population_ratio = if_else(
        population_16plusE > 0,
        employedE / population_16plusE,
        NA_real_
      ),
      labor_force_participation = if_else(
        population_16plusE > 0,
        labor_forceE / population_16plusE,
        NA_real_
      ),
      bachelor_plus_share = if_else(
        population_25plusE > 0,
        (bachelorE + masterE + professionalE + doctorateE) /
          population_25plusE,
        NA_real_
      )
    )
}


get_county_shapes <- function() {

  shapes <- tigris::counties(
    cb = TRUE,
    year = 2024,
    class = "sf"
  )

  # Keep the 50 states and Washington, DC; exclude territories.
  state_fips <- c(
    sprintf("%02d", 1:2),
    sprintf("%02d", 4:6),
    sprintf("%02d", 8:13),
    sprintf("%02d", 15:42),
    sprintf("%02d", 44:51),
    sprintf("%02d", 53:56)
  )

  shapes |>
    filter(STATEFP %in% state_fips) |>
    mutate(GEOID = as.character(GEOID)) |>
    st_transform(5070)
}

# The Geography of America's Industries

Each industry leaves its own geographic footprint. The multi-panel map below shows each major sector’s share of private‑sector county employment. Some industries are widely dispersed across most counties, while others cluster tightly in specific regions.

```{r load-data}
#| label: load-data
#| include: false

qcew_raw <- purrr::map_dfr(
  years,
  function(y) {
    standardize_qcew(download_qcew(y), y)
  }
)

county_shapes <- get_county_shapes()

county_ids <- county_shapes |>
  st_drop_geometry() |>
  distinct(GEOID) |>
  pull(GEOID)

qcew_county <- qcew_raw |>
  filter(
    area_fips %in% county_ids,
    own_code == "5"
  ) |>
  mutate(GEOID = area_fips)

available_codes <- unique(qcew_county$industry_code)

if (!("10" %in% available_codes)) {
  stop(
    "QCEW code 10 (total private) was not found. ",
    "Inspect industry_code in the downloaded file before proceeding."
  )
}

missing_sector_codes <- setdiff(
  sector_codes$industry_code,
  available_codes
)

if (length(missing_sector_codes) > 0) {
  stop(
    "The following expected QCEW sector codes were not found: ",
    paste(missing_sector_codes, collapse = ", "),
    ". Inspect the actual QCEW industry_code values and verify ",
    "the code mapping before proceeding."
  )
}

# Use private ownership consistently for both the denominator and sectors.
private_total <- qcew_county |>
  filter(industry_code == "10") |>
  group_by(year, GEOID) |>
  summarise(
    total_private_employment = first(annual_avg_emplvl),
    .groups = "drop"
  )

# Require a single observation per county-year-sector.
sector_records <- qcew_county |>
  inner_join(sector_codes, by = "industry_code") |>
  group_by(year, GEOID, industry, sector_key) |>
  summarise(
    n_records = n(),
    employment = first(annual_avg_emplvl),
    average_weekly_wage = first(annual_avg_wkly_wage),
    .groups = "drop"
  )

if (any(sector_records$n_records > 1)) {
  stop(
    "Duplicate QCEW county-year-sector observations were detected. ",
    "Inspect ownership and industry aggregation levels before analysis."
  )
}

sector_employment <- sector_records |>
  select(-n_records) |>
  left_join(
    private_total,
    by = c("year", "GEOID")
  ) |>
  mutate(
    employment_share = if_else(
      total_private_employment > 0,
      employment / total_private_employment,
      NA_real_
    )
  )

if (any(
  sector_employment$employment_share > 1.01,
  na.rm = TRUE
)) {
  warning(
    "Some industry employment shares exceed 100%. ",
    "Check QCEW records, aggregation levels, and the denominator."
  )
}

# National location-quotient benchmark using the same county universe.
national_total <- private_total |>
  group_by(year) |>
  summarise(
    national_private_employment = sum(
      total_private_employment,
      na.rm = TRUE
    ),
    .groups = "drop"
  )

national_sector <- sector_employment |>
  group_by(year, industry, sector_key) |>
  summarise(
    national_employment = sum(employment, na.rm = TRUE),
    .groups = "drop"
  ) |>
  left_join(national_total, by = "year") |>
  mutate(
    national_share = national_employment /
      national_private_employment
  )

sector_lq <- sector_employment |>
  left_join(
    national_sector |>
      select(year, industry, sector_key, national_share),
    by = c("year", "industry", "sector_key")
  ) |>
  mutate(
    location_quotient = employment_share / national_share
  )

acs_panel <- map_dfr(years, get_county_acs) |>
  filter(GEOID %in% county_ids)

# The 2019 and 2024 QCEW observations are joined to the corresponding
# ACS 5-year estimates for descriptive comparison.
analysis_panel <- sector_employment |>
  left_join(
    sector_lq |>
      select(year, GEOID, sector_key, location_quotient),
    by = c("year", "GEOID", "sector_key")
  ) |>
  left_join(
    acs_panel,
    by = c("GEOID", "year" = "acs_year")
  )
```

# Where Are People and Skills Concentrated?

We now examine how industries shape a region’s ability to attract residents. Population and human capital reveal the pull of local economies: population size reflects the scale of the labor market, while educational attainment captures formal schooling among residents.
The share of adults aged 25 and older holding a bachelor’s degree or higher acts as our **proxy for one dimension of human capital**. This statistic cannot directly measure practical skills, work experience, labor productivity, or differences in education quality.

# What Do These Patterns Mean for Communities?

Industrial structure shapes local job opportunities, but specialization does not guarantee prosperity. Manufacturing is prominent in many Midwestern and Southern counties, while professional and business services concentrate in metropolitan areas. However, household income also depends on wages, education, labor-market conditions, and population trends. Employment growth from 2019 to 2024 varies across counties and industries. Understanding local economies therefore requires looking beyond dominant industries to assess job access, pay, and long-term opportunities. These patterns show regional differences, not causal relationships.

## Key Takeaways

- **Specialization is not prosperity:** A high location quotient indicates relative industry concentration, not necessarily more jobs or higher household income.
- **Employment, wages, and income measure different things:** Workplace-based job and wage data do not directly capture residents’ financial well-being.
- **People and industries vary across regions:** Population trends, educational attainment, and labor-market conditions provide important context for local economies.
- **Regional growth is uneven:** Manufacturing and professional and business services follow different employment trajectories across counties.

For residents and policymakers, understanding a local economy means asking not only which industries dominate, but also what jobs are available, how they are paid, who benefits, and how opportunities change over time.

For residents and policymakers, the practical question is not simply “Which industry is best?” It is:
**What kinds of jobs are available locally, who can access them, how are they paid, and how is the local mix of opportunities changing?**
