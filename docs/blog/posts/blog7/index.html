Comparing renter housing-cost burdens across 50 states, 2019-2024

Which states saw the largest increase in the share of rent-burdened households between 2019 and 2024? Housing costs matter alongside income: a rent increase need not raise the share of a household’s budget spent on housing if income also rises. The Census Bureau’s housing report describes HUD’s common cost-burden benchmark as spending more than 30% of income on housing. Here, I use at least 30% of household income on rent and utilities, because the first included Census category is 30.0-34.9%. This is a comparison threshold, not a universal test of affordability. Using all 50 states, I compare how the proportion meeting that threshold changed over five years.

Measure the pressure on household budgets

I use the Census Bureau’s ACS 1-year estimates, table B25070, for the same 50 states in both years. Each observation represents a state, not a person. States receive one point each, regardless of their renter populations. DC and Puerto Rico are excluded. The years form a fixed five-year comparison; they are not presented as the latest available data.

Gross rent includes contract rent plus tenant-paid utilities and fuels. The income measure is household income before taxes. I add the four categories from 30% upward and divide by renter households with a computed rent-to-income ratio. The Census definitions exclude no-rent households and those reporting zero or negative income from that computable group. Consequently, the percentages do not cover every renter household.

This measures the prevalence of a budget burden, not the average rent bill. A higher share means that housing takes at least three dollars of every ten in income for a larger fraction of eligible households.

Read the states, then narrow the comparison

The Plotly scatterplot places the 2019 share horizontally and the 2024 share vertically. Above the dashed no-change line means an increase. The vertical difference between a point and that line is the change in percentage points, not percent growth. Both axes keep the same ranges when filtering.

Choose 5 largest estimated increases, then turn on state labels to identify the leading points without the full cluster. Filter a region to see whether it contains any of those five states. Hover to inspect exact shares, household denominators and the approximate change interval. Selecting a state clears the group filters; Reset chart restores all 50.

Where burdens increased most

The largest point-estimate increases are Nevada (6.5 points), Maine (6.3), and Florida (6.2). Arizona and Nebraska complete the top five. Together they span all four Census regions. The increase is therefore not confined to one region in this descriptive ranking.

Nevada rises from 51.1% to 57.6%. Nebraska rises from 41.2% to 46.5%, remaining below half despite its large increase. A large estimated increase therefore need not mean the highest current burden.

Reset and inspect Florida. Its 62.1% is the highest 2024 point estimate, although Nevada has the largest estimated increase. California’s 2024 share is 55.8%. Its increase is 2.6 percentage points, compared with Nevada’s 6.5. A ranking based only on the current level would miss where burdens worsened most.

What not to infer

These are survey estimates. Turn on 90% error bars to see approximate annual uncertainty; hover for the change interval. The Census approximation formulas are documented in the replication guide. These estimates do not establish which state had the largest underlying increase: the intervals are pointwise, not simultaneous ranking intervals.

The surveys also describe different households. Migration, income composition and housing costs could all affect a state’s share, but this table cannot separate their contributions. It cannot establish that an individual renter’s burden increased, explain a policy’s effects, or describe differences between neighborhoods within a state.

Takeaway

Nevada, Maine and Florida have the largest estimated increases, while Florida has the highest 2024 burden share. The share has a higher point estimate in 45 of 50 states; this does not establish an increase in every one of them. Compare changes as well as levels: a state need not start with the highest burden to experience one of the largest increases.
